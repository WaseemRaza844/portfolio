# Teaching Philosophy: From Fundamentals to Engineering Judgment

> Waseem Raza, PhD | Villanova University | Assistant Teaching Professor, Electrical Engineering

## Principles and prior teaching

My goal as an engineering educator is to help students do more than reproduce equations or run software. I want them to **explain a principle, predict system behavior, test that prediction, diagnose discrepancies, and defend a design decision**. An effective classroom connects analytical reasoning to hands-on evidence. When students discover that a real or simulated response differs from the ideal model, they have an opportunity to learn what assumptions mean and how engineers investigate a problem.

I previously taught **Computer Programming, Communication Systems, and Wireless Communication** to undergraduate Electrical Engineering Technology students as a Lecturer in the University of Lahore's Department of Technology. I prepared instructional material, supported evaluation and student projects, and learned to break complex ideas into manageable steps without weakening the technical standard. At the University of Engineering and Technology, Taxila, I supported communications laboratories, coursework, assignments, and grading; at COMSATS University Islamabad, I mentored Bachelor’s and Master’s students on simulation and engineering research projects. These settings reinforced the value of clarity, availability to students, and feedback they can apply immediately.

## Course preparation, circuits and controls

My strongest directly documented teaching preparation is in communications, programming, signals, wireless systems, and computational engineering. Villanova's emphasis on **circuits and control systems** is a purposeful direction for further course development. I would prepare rigorously from established course outcomes, prerequisite maps, laboratory procedures, and faculty collaboration. For introductory circuits, a learning sequence might ask students to derive a first-order transient response, predict a resistor-capacitor circuit's behavior, compare the prediction with a simulation and a bench measurement, and explain the effects of component tolerances or measurement error. For controls, a sequence could move from a transfer-function or state-space model to a step response, stability evaluation, simple controller adjustment, and an explicit discussion of overshoot, settling time, and actuator limits. These are **proposed instructional designs**, not claims that I have previously served as instructor of record for circuits or controls.

I would use simulation to support—not replace—hands-on laboratory experience. MATLAB, Python, C/C++, data acquisition, and clear technical documentation can help students move between models, code, and physical systems. For upper-level or graduate instruction, I could contribute particular depth in communications, signal analysis, data-driven system modeling, and optimization, adapting expectations to the level of the course.

## Assessment, inclusive learning and continuous improvement

I favor frequent low-stakes checks for prerequisite knowledge, worked examples followed by independent variations, and staged assignments that show how a student reasons. In laboratory and design work, I would assess problem formulation, calculations, implementation, evidence, troubleshooting, and communication. A result that is initially incorrect but followed by a sound diagnosis can demonstrate meaningful learning; a correct plot with no explanation may not.

I would define measurable outcomes, use transparent rubrics, collect representative student work, and review recurring gaps with colleagues. For example, if a class repeatedly confuses stability with fast response, I would add a diagnostic comparison of multiple responses and test whether performance improves on a later assignment. This approach supports course assessment and continuous improvement without claiming formal accreditation leadership that is not documented in my prior record.

Students bring different experience with mathematics, programming, English, and laboratory equipment. I would provide clear setup guides, accessible office hours, structured teamwork roles, and opportunities for feedback while maintaining shared expectations. In project teams, each student should demonstrate both an individual technical contribution and an ability to explain team decisions respectfully.

## Advising, capstones and professional formation

Good mentoring begins with listening to the student's goals and helping define an achievable next step. In capstone or independent work, I would encourage students to specify requirements, produce a baseline design, construct a test plan, document risks, and communicate performance and limitations. My project and thesis mentoring at COMSATS and applied experience with AT&T Labs, Futurewei, and AT&T provide a foundation for guiding work in communications, intelligent sensing, and data-driven engineering. I would also support advising on course sequences and professional preparation through attentive consultation with department advisors and program requirements.

Villanova's Augustinian ideals of **truth, unity, and love** are compatible with how I want an engineering classroom to function: be honest about evidence and uncertainty, make space for collaborative learning, and treat each student with dignity. I would encourage students to consider who bears the consequences when an engineered system fails and how technical choices affect the common good. My measure of success is a student who can reason independently, work responsibly with others, and remain curious after graduation.
