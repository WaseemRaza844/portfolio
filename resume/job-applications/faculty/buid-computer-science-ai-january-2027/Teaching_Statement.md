# Teaching Statement — Artificial Intelligence and Computer Science

Waseem Raza, PhD | The British University in Dubai | January 2027

## Teaching philosophy and experience

My teaching philosophy is to connect **conceptual understanding, hands-on implementation, critical evaluation, and professional responsibility**. Students should be able not only to train a model or write a program, but also to explain why an approach works, identify when it fails, and assess its ethical and operational implications. This approach is especially important in AI, where benchmark accuracy can conceal data leakage, distribution shift, inequitable errors, or high deployment costs.

I previously served as Lecturer in the University of Lahore's Department of Technology, teaching Computer Programming, Communication Systems, and Wireless Communication to undergraduates. I supported laboratory instruction, assignments, and assessment in communications at the University of Engineering and Technology, Taxila, and mentored Bachelor’s and Master’s research and thesis-related projects at COMSATS University Islamabad. These experiences informed my use of structured worked examples, practical exercises, transparent rubrics, and responsive feedback. I have also worked in interdisciplinary and multicultural academic and industry environments.

## Undergraduate and postgraduate AI instruction

I would contribute most strongly to **Introduction to AI, Machine Learning, Deep Learning, Applied Data Science, Python Programming, Neural Networks, Intelligent Systems, and AI Research Methods**, according to programme needs and course preparation. I can also support application-oriented subjects in network intelligence, time-series analytics, responsible AI, and generative AI. My prior instructor-of-record subjects are programming and communications; the AI courses listed here are proposed teaching assignments grounded in research and industrial expertise, not claimed prior faculty appointments.

In an undergraduate machine-learning module, students could progress from regression and classification to feature engineering, cross-validation, interpretability, and a reproducible final project. A practical laboratory might compare a simple classifier and a compact neural network on sensor data, then test robustness against missing samples or a new user. At MSc level, students could analyse a research paper, reproduce a published result, investigate limitations, and propose a defensible extension. For generative AI, learning objectives would include grounding, retrieval, evaluation, privacy, and the distinction between plausible output and verified evidence.

## Inclusive, technology-enhanced learning and assessment

I would use a structured combination of short concept checks, guided notebooks, live demonstrations, staged project deliverables, and peer discussion. Jupyter notebooks, Git-based version control, simulation tools, and well-documented datasets can make experimentation repeatable; accessible setup instructions and alternative routes to participation help students with different computing backgrounds. I would assess both individual understanding and teamwork through rubrics covering problem formulation, implementation quality, experimental design, error analysis, communication, and responsible use of data.

Continuous improvement should be evidence-driven. I would map major assignments to programme learning outcomes, examine patterns of errors, solicit student feedback, and revise instruction accordingly. For example, if students score well on model training but struggle with leakage or unreliable evaluation, I would strengthen the experimental-design component and check improvement in a subsequent assignment. I would participate in programme quality assurance and ABET-related processes as assigned, without overstating prior accreditation responsibilities.

## Student research supervision and development

My mentoring approach begins with a tractable research question and a reproducible baseline, followed by planned milestones and regular feedback. Undergraduate capstones could investigate robust wearable-sensor classification or AI-based network anomaly detection. MSc dissertations could examine domain adaptation, data-efficient generative learning, or uncertainty-aware inference. For doctoral co-supervision, I would contribute expertise in machine-learning methodology, robust evaluation, and applied intelligent systems in collaboration with established faculty supervisors. Project documentation, ethical data handling, reproducibility, and authorship transparency would be integral throughout.

I also value workshops and community engagement. Short industry-oriented sessions on Python for AI, machine-learning evaluation, and responsible generative AI could connect BUiD's programmes with working professionals. My aim is to prepare graduates who can create useful AI systems, question their limitations, and continue learning as the field changes.
