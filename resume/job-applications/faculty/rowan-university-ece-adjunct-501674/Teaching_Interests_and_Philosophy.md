## Teaching philosophy: analytical foundations through implementation

I believe engineering students understand complex systems best when mathematical analysis, working examples and design decisions reinforce one another. My classes would progress from clear learning objectives and concise derivations to software-based experiments and interpretation of results. In communications, students might vary signal-to-noise ratio and compare bit-error-rate predictions with simulations; in DSP, they might design and test filters on a measured signal; in image processing, they might compare enhancement methods; and in ML, they might reproduce a baseline and diagnose errors. Each activity would culminate in an explanation of **why** the method works, its assumptions and limitations, not just a successful plot or code run.

The approach is grounded in my prior teaching at the University of Lahore and communications laboratory support at UET Taxila. Students arrive with varied backgrounds in mathematics, programming and circuit theory. I would start with diagnostic questions and scaffolded tasks, provide worked examples and accessible office hours, and progressively remove scaffolds as learners develop confidence. Rubrics would assess conceptual understanding, implementation correctness, experimental reasoning, teamwork and technical communication.

## Experiential learning, assessment and inclusion

Rowan's engineering-clinic culture and lab/project integration match my interest in hands-on, inquiry-driven teaching. I would use small, achievable teams and clearly defined individual contributions; Git-based version control, reproducible notebooks or MATLAB scripts, structured lab reports, and short design reviews where appropriate. Example projects would rely on available department resources and approved safety practices rather than assuming access to specific equipment.

I would combine low-stakes checks for understanding with laboratory milestones, programming assignments, midterm evaluations and a culminating application. Frequent feedback would help students correct misconceptions early; multiple examples and accessible instructions would support students with different academic preparation. Students should be able to articulate tradeoffs, evaluate results honestly, and identify when a method or model is unreliable.

My doctoral research involved wireless simulation, data-efficient ML, and network reliability. I would translate those experiences into de-identified or synthetic student datasets and assignments, never proprietary or sensitive operational records.

My goal is for students to leave each course able to **derive key ideas, build and test working implementations, interpret engineering evidence, and communicate reliable conclusions**. I would be glad to develop the specific assignments and project scope collaboratively with Rowan's ECE faculty.
