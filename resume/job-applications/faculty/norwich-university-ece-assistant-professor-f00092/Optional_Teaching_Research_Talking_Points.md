# Optional Teaching and Research Talking Points

> Waseem Raza, PhD | Norwich University ECE | F00092

## Teaching and senior design

My teaching connects engineering theory, computation, laboratory experimentation, and interpretation. My prior university instruction includes Computer Programming, Communication Systems, and Wireless Communication, with additional laboratory support and research mentorship. I would contribute to those subjects, signals and systems, DSP, networking, and applied AI/ML, and prepare other core ECE courses against the approved syllabus and available laboratory resources. I would coordinate with hardware and cybersecurity specialists when developing microcontroller, embedded-system or security-oriented activities that require expertise beyond my established teaching record.

A prospective senior design project could compare a baseline sensor classifier with an ML model on open inertial data, quantify false alarms and variation across users, and document tests and limitations. A second project could simulate packet loss or network outages, evaluate detection and recovery methods, and investigate how faulty or manipulated measurements affect the outcome. In both cases teams would define requirements, record design decisions, test alternatives, explain failure modes, and communicate results. These activities provide useful entry points into secure and resilient engineering without claiming direct prior hardware-security instruction.

For assessment and continuous improvement, I would align assignments and lab rubrics with course outcomes, document student attainment, discuss patterns with colleagues, and revise instruction based on evidence. I would participate in program-level ABET assessment according to Norwich procedures. Advising would connect technical coursework to internships, capstones, career opportunities and graduate study.

## Undergraduate scholarship

My research program would focus on data-efficient and robust machine learning, intelligent sensing, and reliable networked systems. Early projects would use open or synthetic datasets and existing simulation tools. Students could reproduce baselines, evaluate distribution shifts, investigate uncertainty and false alarms, and develop reproducible tools. More advanced students could explore adaptation, selective prediction, and integration with physical or networked prototypes in collaboration with relevant faculty.

In the first year I would establish a small benchmark and project repository, recruit students through courses and independent research, and develop realistic milestones. In subsequent years I would pursue peer-reviewed publications, student coauthorship and external proposals appropriate to the research scope. No funding award or existing external partnership is assumed. New human-subject research would require institutional approval and consent.
