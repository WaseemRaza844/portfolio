# Teaching Philosophy: Engineering Fundamentals, Experimentation and Responsible AI

> Waseem Raza, PhD | Rutgers ECE Assistant Professor | 26FA1036

## Teaching philosophy and experience

My goal is to help ECE students move from understanding a model to testing it in an engineering setting and explaining why its predictions do or do not agree with observations. A student who can derive an equation but cannot interrogate a surprising measurement has not yet completed the learning process. I therefore structure technical learning around **concept, prediction, implementation, evidence and interpretation**, with opportunities for feedback throughout.

I previously taught Computer Programming, Communication Systems and Wireless Communication at the University of Lahore. At the University of Engineering and Technology, Taxila, I supported communications laboratories, instructional materials, assignments and grading. At COMSATS University Islamabad and the University of Oklahoma, I mentored student research involving engineering computation, wireless simulations, machine-learning experiments and technical presentations. These experiences have taught me to clarify prerequisites, provide accessible entry points and use direct feedback to support students with varied preparation.

## Undergraduate and graduate courses

At Rutgers, I would contribute most immediately to programming, communication systems, wireless networking, introductory AI/ML applications, engineering computation, and project-based laboratory instruction. My doctoral and applied research also prepare me to develop teaching in signals and systems, digital signal processing, intelligent sensing and advanced network intelligence. At the graduate level, I could offer advanced topics in AI for wireless and networked systems, data-efficient learning and reliable sensing. For any core course outside my previous instructor-of-record experience, I would prepare against the approved curriculum, prerequisites and laboratory resources in consultation with colleagues.

I favor exercises that expose differences between theoretical and real-world assumptions. In a communication systems laboratory, students could derive a path-loss estimate, simulate link performance under different environments, and account for measurement error. In signal processing, students could predict the effect of changing a sample rate or filter, then inspect time- and frequency-domain outputs in MATLAB or Python. In an applied machine-learning elective, students could compare interpretable baselines with neural classifiers and evaluate failure under dataset shift. These are *prospective instructional examples*, not claims that I previously taught those exact laboratories.

## Assessment, inclusion and responsible AI use

I would make learning objectives measurable: explain a technical choice, derive a model, implement a test, evaluate uncertainty or communicate a system tradeoff. Low-stakes concept questions, scaffolded assignments and short laboratory checkpoints would reveal misunderstandings before major examinations. For projects, rubrics would distinguish design reasoning, implementation, verification, analysis and communication. I would retain evidence of student learning and review patterns with faculty colleagues to improve courses over time.

Students differ in familiarity with mathematics, laboratory instruments, software environments and technical English. I would provide tested setup instructions, clear documentation, structured group responsibilities and accessible office hours while maintaining common expectations. Students who understand why a solution failed often gain more enduring confidence than students who obtain a correct output without understanding it.

Generative AI tools can support programming, documentation and exploratory debugging, but students must remain accountable for reasoning, correctness and academic integrity. Where permitted by university policy, I would teach students to verify AI-generated code against tests, report tool use transparently and avoid transmitting sensitive data into unapproved systems. Independent examinations, code walkthroughs and individual oral explanations can help distinguish student understanding from unexamined automated output.

## Mentoring, advising and service

I would guide capstone and independent research teams through manageable milestones: a defined problem, baseline, design alternatives, prototype, validation and report. I would seek opportunities to connect students with open-data projects and industry-informed case studies, while protecting confidential operational information. My experience across university research, wireless R&D and machine-learning engineering can help students understand how signal processing, coding, networks and applied AI translate into professional pathways.

As a Rutgers faculty member, I would participate in undergraduate and graduate advising, curriculum development, committee and professional service, and constructive assessment of program outcomes. I aim to build rigorous and supportive learning environments in which students leave with mathematical foundations, engineering judgment, communication skills and the confidence to learn unfamiliar technologies.
